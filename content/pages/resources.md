You don't need to be an engineer to fix bad lighting in your community. You need a flashlight, a camera, and a one-page letter. Here's the toolkit.

## 1. Audit your own street

- **Look up.** Can you see the Milky Way? If not, what's glowing — a specific fixture, a parking lot, a ballpark?
- **Look sideways.** Find every light where you can see the bare bulb from across the street. That's glare — it's the fixture failing, not your eyes.
- **Look at your bedroom at midnight.** Is any light spilling in? Note the source and the time.
- **Note the color.** Is it warm amber or harsh blue-white? Color temperature is the single biggest predictor of how much a fixture pollutes.

Take photos and write down the make/model if visible. A specific complaint beats a general one every time.

## 2. Write the letter (copy-paste starter)

> Dear [Council member / Public Works director],
>
> I'm writing about the street lighting on [street / area]. The current fixtures are [unshielded / 4000K+ / always at full output at 3 a.m.]. This creates glare for drivers, light trespass into homes, and skyglow that hides the night sky, while wasting energy and harming local wildlife.
>
> Modern, fully-shielded, 3000K-or-warmer fixtures with after-hours dimming would cost less to run, improve visibility, and reduce light pollution. Will the [city/county] adopt a dark-sky lighting standard for new and replacement fixtures?
>
> Thank you, [name, address]

## 3. The one-page standard to ask for

- **≤3000K** color temperature for all public outdoor lighting.
- **Full shielding** — zero uplight, flat lens.
- **Dim to ≤50%** from midnight to 5 a.m.
- **Lumen caps** tied to road classification, not vendor default.

This is, nearly word for word, the guidance of the **International Dark-Sky Association** — and dozens of municipalities (Flagstaff, AZ; the island of Sark; and many others) have adopted versions of it without losing safety.

## 4. Model ordinances & allies

- **International Dark-Sky Association (IDA)** — `darksky.org` — the central resource: model lighting ordinances, fixture seal of approval, and a growing list of certified Dark Sky Places.
- **IDA's "Model Lighting Ordinance"** — a ready-to-adapt legal template you can hand to your city attorney.
- **Your local astronomy club** — they've been fighting this for decades and almost always have someone who'll join you at a council meeting.

## 5. Talk like it's about safety and money

City staff tune out "save the stars." They listen to "saves money," "reduces glare for drivers," and "fewer complaints from residents." Lead with those. The night sky is the bonus.

Change starts with one shielded fixture on one street. Be the person who asks for it.
