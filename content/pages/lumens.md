title: Lumens, brightness, and why more isn't better
visibility: public

If you take one thing from this site, make it this: **lumens measure how much light a fixture puts out, not how well you can see.** Brightness and visibility are two different things.

## The vocabulary, in plain English

- **Lumen (lm)** — the total amount of light a bulb emits in every direction. A single candle is roughly 12 lumens; a classic 60-watt incandescent bulb is about 800 lumens.
- **Lux** — lumens *per square meter*. Lux is what actually reaches a surface. A 10,000-lumen lamp pointed at the sky puts close to zero useful lux on the ground where you need it.
- **Candela** — intensity in a single direction. A bare bulb glaring straight into your eye can have enormous candela and terrible usefulness.
- **Color temperature (Kelvin, K)** — how "warm" or "cool" the light *looks*. Warm amber is below 3000K; neutral white is 3500–4000K; harsh blue-white "daylight" is above 4000K. The higher the Kelvin, the more blue light it emits — and the worse it is for people and wildlife at night.

## What's actually on your street

A typical modern street light runs **1,000 to 4,000 lumens**. That's not inherently evil. The problem is threefold:

1. **It points everywhere.** An unshielded "cobra-head" or globe fixture throws a large share of its lumens up and sideways — lighting the sky, not the road.
2. **It's too blue.** Many municipal LEDs are 4000K or 5000K. Blue-rich light scatters far more in the atmosphere (that's why the daytime sky is blue), so it drives skyglow out of proportion to its brightness.
3. **It's on when nobody needs it.** Full output at 3 a.m. on an empty street wastes energy and does real biological harm for zero benefit.

## The fix is measurement, not darkness

The goal is not fewer lumens — it's the *right* lumens, *aimed* at the ground, in a *warm* color, *dimmed* when the street is empty. A fully-shielded 3000K fixture at 2,000 lumens can light a road better than a 4,000K unshielded fixture at 4,000 lumens, while polluting a fraction of the sky.

When you hear a city claim "new LEDs are brighter and safer," ask a better question: **how many of those lumens are landing on the road, and what color are they?**
